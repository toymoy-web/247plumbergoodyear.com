Garbage Disposal Keeps Jamming? Here's What's Actually Causing It

By the Goodyear 24/7 Plumbing team.

Call Now

A garbage disposal that hums without spinning, or one that trips repeatedly, is one of the more common kitchen calls we get, and the good news is that the cause is almost always one of a small number of things. Most of the time, it's fixable in minutes once you know what to check.

The Humming-But-Not-Spinning Jam.

This is the most common symptom, and it almost always means the impeller plate inside the disposal is physically stuck against something, usually a fibrous piece of food like celery, corn husk, or a fruit pit that's wedged between the plate and the grinding ring. The motor is trying to run but can't turn against the obstruction, which is exactly what the humming sound is.

Most disposals have a small hex-shaped hole on the bottom of the unit, underneath the sink, designed for exactly this situation. Insert a hex key or an disposal wrench, if one came with the unit, and turn it firmly in both directions to manually work the impeller plate free. Once it turns freely by hand, try the disposal again, and it will often run normally.

What Actually Causes The Jam In The First Place.

Fibrous vegetables like celery and corn husks are frequent culprits, since their long strings can wrap around the grinding components rather than breaking down cleanly. Fruit pits and large bones are hard enough to lodge against the impeller plate without breaking. Coffee grounds and eggshells don't jam the mechanism directly but contribute to a sludge buildup that makes jamming more likely over time.

Grease and cooking oil are a less obvious cause. They coat the inside of the disposal and the drain line beyond it, and as they cool and solidify, they narrow the passage and slow drainage, which can make an otherwise minor jam feel like a bigger clog than it actually is.

When It's Not A Jam At All.

If the disposal makes no sound whatsoever, not even a hum, the issue is more likely electrical: a tripped reset button on the bottom of the unit, a tripped circuit breaker, or in some cases a failed switch. The reset button, a small red button on the underside of most disposals, is worth pressing firmly before assuming anything more serious is wrong.

If the unit hums but the reset button doesn't help and manually freeing the impeller plate doesn't either, that points toward a failed motor rather than a simple jam. That's the point where repair or replacement becomes the more realistic conversation rather than another attempt to clear it yourself.

Leaks Are A Different Problem With A Different Fix.

A disposal leaking water underneath the sink is not related to jamming at all, and it's worth not confusing the two. Leaks usually trace back to a worn seal where the disposal connects to the sink flange, a loose connection at the dishwasher drain line, or a cracked housing on an older unit. Each of these has its own specific fix, and none of them are solved by clearing a jam.

How To Avoid Repeat Jams.

Running cold water, not hot, while the disposal is operating helps solidify grease so it gets ground up and flushed through rather than coating the inside of the unit. Avoid feeding fibrous vegetables, large quantities of food at once, or anything genuinely hard like bones or fruit pits into the disposal at all, even though it's tempting to treat it as a catch-all for kitchen scraps.

If jams are becoming frequent despite reasonable use, that's often a sign the unit itself is aging and losing torque, which makes it more prone to stalling on food that a newer, stronger motor would grind through without issue. At that point, a repair versus replacement conversation, based on the unit's age and how often it's failing, makes more sense than repeatedly clearing the same recurring jam.

How Long A Disposal Should Actually Last.

A well-maintained garbage disposal typically lasts 8 to 15 years, though hard water across Goodyear can push units toward the shorter end of that range, since mineral buildup on internal components adds wear beyond normal use. If your unit is original to the home and you're not sure of its age, checking the manufacturer's label for a date code is worth doing. That's especially true before investing in another repair on a unit that may be near the natural end of its service life anyway.

What A Repair Visit Actually Involves.

Beyond manually freeing a stuck impeller plate, a repair visit checks the electrical connection, tests the reset button and any accessible wiring for a fault, and inspects seals if there's any sign of leaking alongside the jamming. Most disposal repairs are completed in well under an hour, since the mechanism itself is relatively simple compared to other plumbing appliances.

If the motor has genuinely failed rather than just jammed, replacement is usually the more sensible path, since disposal motors aren't typically repaired as an isolated component the way a larger appliance motor might be. A new unit installation is usually completed same visit, often using the existing mounting and electrical connection if it's still in good condition.

Choosing A Replacement Unit.

Horsepower ratings for residential disposals typically range from a third of a horsepower up to one full horsepower, with higher ratings handling tougher food waste and larger households with less frequent jamming. Noise-reduction features, sound insulation built into the unit's housing, are worth considering if your kitchen sink is near a living or dining area where disposal noise is noticeable during use, particularly in an open-concept layout.
