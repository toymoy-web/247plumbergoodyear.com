Why Your Water Heater Dies Faster in Goodyear Than the National Average

By the Goodyear 24/7 Plumbing team.

Call Now

A water heater is generally rated for 10 to 15 years of service. In Goodyear, we replace plenty of units well before they hit that range, and the reason almost always traces back to the same underlying cause: mineral content in the water supply settling inside the tank faster than the manufacturer's testing accounted for.

How Sediment Actually Shortens A Water Heater's Life.

Hard water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium that settle out as sediment at the bottom of a tank over time. That layer acts as insulation between the burner or heating element and the water above it, forcing the unit to work harder and longer to reach the same temperature. The popping or rumbling sound many homeowners notice as a water heater ages is literally the sound of water boiling underneath that sediment layer.

Beyond the noise, sediment buildup accelerates corrosion at the tank's base, which is the point of failure in most water heater leaks. A unit running on untreated hard water in Goodyear commonly shows this wear years before an identical model would in a soft-water region, simply because the sediment accumulation rate is that much faster here.

Why This Affects Every Home, Not Just Older Ones.

Because the water hardness itself doesn't change based on when a home was built, a water heater installed last year in a Palm Valley new-build faces the same sediment accumulation rate as one in a decades-old home south of I-10. What differs is how much total time has passed, not whether the underlying mechanism applies. A newer home just hasn't accumulated the effects yet.

Arizona's summer heat compounds this in a less obvious way. Extreme ambient temperatures during the hottest months put additional thermal stress on a water heater's components regardless of water hardness, and a unit already dealing with sediment buildup has less margin to absorb that extra strain before something fails.

Annual Flushing Helps, But It's Not A Complete Fix.

Flushing a water heater once a year removes loose sediment before it hardens into a thick, insulating layer, and it's a genuinely useful maintenance step. But flushing addresses what has already accumulated, not the ongoing mineral content in every gallon of water flowing through the unit between flushes. It slows the problem without removing its actual source, which is why units on an annual flushing schedule still eventually need replacement earlier than the manufacturer's stated lifespan would suggest.

What A Softener Changes.

A correctly sized whole-home water softener removes the minerals before they ever reach the water heater, which addresses the cause directly rather than managing the accumulation after the fact. Homes we service with a properly sized softener in place consistently go longer between water heater repairs and replacements than comparable homes running on untreated water, which is the most direct evidence that the mineral content, not just general wear, is the driving factor.

Softener sizing depends on household size and the actual hardness level at your specific address, since hardness varies somewhat across Goodyear's four water utilities. A softener sized for a national average doesn't necessarily match what your home's water actually needs, which is why we test rather than assume before recommending any specific unit or capacity.

What This Means For Your Next Water Heater.

If you're replacing a water heater that failed early, it's worth asking whether a softener installed alongside the new unit would meaningfully extend its life before you're back in the same position in five years instead of twelve. It's an additional upfront cost, but one that's generally smaller than the cost of an early replacement it's likely to prevent.

Signs Your Current Unit Is Already Struggling With Sediment.

A popping, cracking, or rumbling sound during the heating cycle is the clearest early sign, caused by water trapped and boiling underneath accumulated sediment. Reduced hot water capacity, where the unit seems to run out faster than it used to for the same household usage, is another common symptom, since sediment takes up tank volume that used to hold usable water.

Rust-colored water at the start of a hot water draw, which clears after a few seconds, often indicates the anode rod inside the tank is deteriorating faster than normal, frequently accelerated by the same hard water conditions driving the sediment buildup. None of these signs alone means immediate failure is coming, but together they're worth a diagnostic visit rather than waiting for a complete breakdown, particularly once a unit has passed the halfway point of its expected service life.

How Often This Comes Up On Service Calls.

Water heater leak and water heater repair calls together represent a significant share of what we're dispatched for across Goodyear in any given month, and hard water sediment is the single most common underlying cause we find once we're actually inspecting the unit. It's a more consistent factor than the specific brand, install quality, or even the home's age in isolation, which is unusual for a plumbing problem where causes are often more varied.

That consistency is exactly why we bring up softening proactively rather than waiting for a homeowner to ask. A water heater is a large enough expense that addressing the root cause is worth it. It tends to save more money over time than repeatedly repairing or replacing units running on the same untreated water year after year, rather than just treating the symptom in front of us on a given call.
